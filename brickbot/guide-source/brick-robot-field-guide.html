How to use this guide

  • Work through the phases in order, top to bottom. Each phase ends where the next begins.
  • Every numbered step is one small action. Do the step. Then read the next one.
  • Anything that looks like this is typed into the Terminal app (macOS: press ⌘-Space, type "Terminal", press Enter). Copy the whole block, paste it, press Enter.
  • Everything runs from the Terminal with copy-paste commands — each step below tells you exactly what to type and what you should see. (LeLab, Hugging Face's GUI app, exists as an alternative, but this guide does not use it.)
  • Values that appear in many commands: your two USB port names (found in Phase 2, shown in orange placeholders — replace them), your Hugging Face username (vik101, used to log in), and the team org (sammy-labs — where datasets, policies, and the training credit live). Because other teams share the org, everything this project pushes there is prefixed brickbot_ — that prefix is our territory marker; never push an unprefixed repo.
  • Yellow boxes are warnings — read them before doing the steps next to them. Blue boxes are helpful context — read them once.

Time to expect: one weekend to build the arms and drive them by hand (Phases 0–3). One more day to record data and train your first policy (Phases 4–6). Then weeks of enjoyable iteration — that loop is where the real learning happens. A first policy that only works when everything is exactly like training is a success. That is what this type of model does.

Setup & build
First policy

Reality check: the SO-101 will never lift a real brick — and that's fine

One number matters before anything else. The SO-101 can lift roughly 200–500 grams. A standard clay brick weighs 1.9–2.3 kilograms — four to eight times too heavy. The brick is also 92 mm wide, which does not fit the gripper.

So what is the SO-101 for?

It is your training ground. Every commercial brick robot (Hadrian X, SAM100, Monumental) works from CAD plans and precise position control — none of them uses learned AI policies. So the skills you build here are genuinely new territory. Everything you learn on the SO-101 — collecting demonstrations, training policies, evaluating them — transfers directly to a bigger arm later. The plan: miniature bricks on the SO-101 now, a ~$2,500 arm with a 1.5 kg payload for real lightweight masonry later, and a $15k+ arm for standard clay bricks only if you get that far and still want it. Full plan in the brick curriculum.

How the robot learns — 3 minutes of theory

The method is called imitation learning. You show the robot the task about 50 times by driving it with the leader arm. Cameras record what the robot sees. The motors record how they moved. Then a neural network learns the pattern: "when the cameras see this, the motors should do that."

The trained policy then replaces you. At each moment it looks at: the two camera images and the six current motor angles. Nothing else — no memory, no history. It outputs a short plan of future motor angles, called a chunk:

The policy you will train is SmolVLA — Hugging Face's 450-million-parameter vision-language-action model, pre-trained on community robot data and fine-tuned on your demonstrations from the lerobot/smolvla_base checkpoint. Small enough to train and serve cheaply, big enough to read your task sentence and generalize. Three facts that explain most of what you will observe:

  • It starts smart, and your demos specialize it. Unlike a from-scratch model, SmolVLA already understands objects, language, and manipulation in general; your episodes teach it your specific arm, cameras, and task. It generalizes better than small models — but coverage still comes from varied episodes, not from more training steps.
  • Consistency still beats quantity. Inconsistent demonstrations still teach a muddled behavior. Pick one style. Repeat it every episode.
  • The cameras and your task sentence are its inputs. It genuinely reads the instruction — with a language-conditioned model, the task sentence describes the goal, and consistency of wording between recording and rollout matters. If you can't do the task from the camera images alone, neither can the model.

Warning: old instructions are everywhere

LeRobot changed a lot in 2025–2026. Most tutorials on the internet — including the videos and the ADVANCED-robotics repo you cloned — show commands that no longer exist. That repo is by Trelis Research, the maker of the three videos. Its advice is excellent. Its commands are from mid-2025 and many rely on his personal fork of LeRobot. (A modernized copy of his Training docs now lives on the modernize-lerobot-v0.6 branch of that repo.)

Phase 0 · PreparationPrepare and sort the kit

Goal of this phase: parts sorted, dangers understood, extra equipment ordered. You will not assemble anything yet — the motors need software first (Phase 1), because each one must be programmed before it goes into the arm (Phase 2).

  1. Clear a workspace.

    You need about 1 m × 1 m of desk, near your computer, with room to clamp the follower arm down later. This desk becomes the robot's permanent home — the cameras must never move once you start recording.

  2. Sort the parts into two piles: follower and leader.

    The follower has a gripper (two pincers). The leader has a handle with a trigger. Each arm has 6 servos, one control board, one USB-C cable, and one power supply.

  3. Check your power supplies and servo labels.
    This can destroy servos — read carefully The servos come in two voltage versions. Look at the sticker on each servo — it states the voltage. Count your power supplies.
    • Two different supplies (5V and 12V): your follower has 12V servos (stronger) and your leader has 7.4V servos. The leader always gets the 5V supply. The follower always gets the 12V supply. This applies to every step in this guide, including calibration. 12V into the leader permanently kills its servos.
    • Identical supplies: all 12 servos are the 7.4V version. Both arms use the same supply. No danger.

    Label the supplies with tape right now: "LEADER" and "FOLLOWER".

  4. Check the leader's servo gear labels.

    The leader's six servos are not identical — they have different internal gears so the arm moves freely in your hand. Each has a small code on its label. During assembly, each code must go into its specific joint:

    Label codeGoes into (leader only)
    C001Shoulder lift (joint 2)
    C044Shoulder pan (joint 1) and elbow (joint 3)
    C046Wrist flex (4), wrist roll (5), gripper trigger (6)

    The follower's six servos are all the same — no sorting needed there.

  5. Find a data-capable USB-C cable (or two).

    Charge-only cables are a classic silent failure: the arm powers on but the computer never sees it. If a cable came with the kit, use that one. If you supply your own, test it later by checking that the port-finding tool in Phase 2 sees the board.

  6. Order the extra equipment.
    • Front camera: already sorted ✓ — the Acer 1080p webcam (generic UVC, plug-and-play).
    • Wrist camera: check the kit's included camera first — if it's a small bare board camera, use it on the wrist. If it's a full-size webcam, buy a small wide-angle (~130°) fixed-focus UVC board camera (~$30, e.g. InnoMaker). Light and small matters: every gram on the wrist eats payload.
    • A powered USB hub — you will plug in 4 devices (2 arms, 2 cameras).
    • A clamp to fix the follower's base to the desk.
    • A wrist-camera mount — 3D-print one from the SO-ARM100 repo, or improvise with a small bracket. It must hold the camera rigidly.
    • First "bricks": already sorted ✓ — your wooden stacking-tower (Jenga-style) blocks. They're near-ideal brick proxies: brick-shaped, ~10–20 g (far inside the payload budget), ~2.5 cm wide (fits the gripper), and rigid with real sliding friction — better training objects than foam. Two notes: smooth wood is slippery, so grasp across the narrow face and squeeze decisively; and pick ONE block to be "the brick" for your first dataset — identical-looking spares are your friends when one gets knocked off the table. Later stages: 1:12-scale kiln-fired clay miniature bricks (cheap on Amazon / miniature-craft shops).
  7. Bookmark the official assembly guide.

    It is here, with a video for every joint: huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/so101. You will follow it in Phase 2. Tools you'll need: precision Phillips #0/#1 screwdrivers and a small flathead. Budget 1–2 hours per arm.

Phase 1 · SoftwareInstall the software ✓ completed 2026-08-04

Goal of this phase: LeRobot installed and verified, and your free online accounts created. Everything happens in the Terminal. This phase is done on your Mac — LeRobot 0.6.1 in conda env lerobot (Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.11 with Apple-GPU support), logged in to Hugging Face as vik101. The steps remain below for reference or for setting up another machine.

  1. Install Miniconda (a Python manager).

    If you already have conda (type conda --version to check), skip this step. Otherwise, on a Mac with Homebrew:

    brew install miniforge

    No Homebrew? Download the installer from conda-forge.org/download, run it, then close and reopen the Terminal. Afterwards run conda init zsh once so conda activate works in new terminals.

  2. Create the LeRobot environment.

    This makes a clean box named "lerobot" for all the robot software:

    conda create -y -n lerobot python=3.12
  3. Activate the environment. 
    conda activate lerobot

    Remember this command. You must run it in every new Terminal window before any lerobot-… command. If a command ever says "command not found", this is almost always why.

  4. Install ffmpeg (video encoder). 
    conda install -y -c conda-forge ffmpeg
  5. Install LeRobot.

    The extras in brackets matter: feetech talks to your servos, core_scripts provides record/teleop, training provides the trainer.

    pip install 'lerobot[core_scripts,training,feetech]'
  6. Verify the install. 
    lerobot-info

    It should print version information without errors. If it errors, see Troubleshooting.

  7. Create a free Hugging Face account.

    Go to huggingface.co/join. Your datasets and trained policies will live there. Note your username.

  8. Create an access token.

    On huggingface.co: click your avatar → Settings → Access Tokens → Create new token. Choose type Write. Copy the token (it looks like hf_…).

  9. Log in from the Terminal. 
    hf auth login

    Paste the token when asked (pasting shows nothing on screen — that's normal). Press Enter.

  10. Save your username as a shortcut.

    This lets later commands say ${HF_USER} instead of your username (yours is vik101). Run it in every new Terminal window (or type vik101 by hand later):

    HF_USER=$(NO_COLOR=1 hf auth whoami | awk -F': *' 'NR==1 {print $2}')
echo $HF_USER   # should print vik101

Phase 2 · BuildSet up motors, assemble, calibrate

Goal of this phase: both arms built, every servo programmed, both arms calibrated. This is the longest phase — take a full afternoon per arm.

Which computer? Always the Mac The robot only ever connects to your Mac — every hardware step in Phases 2–4 and 6 happens there. Training (Phase 5) doesn't happen on any of your machines at all: it runs on a rented cloud GPU via Hugging Face Jobs, which receives the dataset and returns the policy through the Hub.

2a. Find your USB ports

  1. Connect the follower's control board to the Mac.

    Plug its USB-C cable into the Mac and its power supply (the FOLLOWER-labeled one!) into the wall. No servos need to be connected yet.

  2. Run the port finder. 
    lerobot-find-port

    It asks you to unplug the USB cable. Unplug it. It then prints the port name — something like /dev/tty.usbmodem58760431551. Plug the cable back in.

  3. Write the port name down. Label it "FOLLOWER PORT".

    You will paste it into almost every command from now on. Keep a note open with both port names.

  4. Repeat steps 1–3 with the leader's board.

    Label the result "LEADER PORT". Tip: keep each board plugged into the same physical USB socket from now on — port names can change if you move plugs around.

2b. Program the motor IDs (before assembly)

Each servo must be told its number (1–6) once, and this must happen before the servos are built into the arm — the tool works on one servo at a time.

  1. Connect ONE follower servo to the follower board.

    Use one 3-pin cable. Any servo — the tool will tell you which joint it becomes. If your board is the Waveshare type, first check its two jumpers are on channel B.

  2. Run the motor setup tool for the follower. 
    lerobot-setup-motors \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT
  3. Follow the prompts.

    It says: "Connect the controller board to the gripper motor only and press Enter." That means: the servo currently plugged in becomes the gripper (ID 6). Press Enter. Then it asks for the next motor — unplug the current servo, plug in the next one, press Enter. It works down from gripper (6) to shoulder pan (1). Put a sticky label with the number on each servo as it's programmed.

  4. Repeat for the leader — with the right servo at each prompt.

    The leader's servos differ per joint (gear codes from Phase 0, step 4 — printed on each servo's sticker). Sort them into three piles first (1× C001, 2× C044, 3× C046 — if a label is unreadable, the C046s twist most freely by hand, the C001 resists most), then plug in the right one as the tool asks:

    Tool asks forIDPlug in a…
    Gripper (trigger)6C046
    Wrist roll5C046
    Wrist flex4C046
    Elbow3C044
    Shoulder lift2C001
    Shoulder pan1C044
    lerobot-setup-motors \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=LEADER_PORT
  5. Now assemble both arms.

    Follow the official guide joint by joint. Each numbered servo goes into its joint (1 = shoulder pan at the base … 6 = gripper at the tip). Connect the servos in a chain with the 3-pin cables — each servo to the next, and servo 1 to the control board — as in the diagram above. Ignore any instruction anywhere about removing gears (that's the old SO-100).

  6. Clamp the follower's base to the desk.

    It will fight its own movements otherwise.

2c. Calibrate both arms

Calibration teaches the software each joint's true range. You do it once per arm. It takes two minutes.

  1. Power and connect both arms.

    Correct power supply for each (check the tape labels!), USB into data ports. In a Terminal: conda activate lerobot.

  2. Start the follower's calibration.

    The id names this arm's calibration file — pick it once and never change it. (Your port values are in the repo's SETUP-LOG.md.)

    lerobot-calibrate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT \
    --robot.id=brick_follower
  3. Hold the L-pose, press Enter.

    Upright from the base, forearm horizontal, gripper down and closed — every joint near mid-range (diagram above).

  4. Sweep every joint to both ends, then press Enter.

    One joint at a time, gently to both stops: gripper, wrist roll, wrist flex, elbow, shoulder lift, shoulder pan. The numbers on screen record each range. When all six have visited both ends, press Enter to save.

  5. Repeat for the leader. 
    lerobot-calibrate \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=LEADER_PORT \
    --teleop.id=brick_leader

    Same pose, same sweep; the leader's "gripper" is its trigger — squeeze it fully open and closed. Files land in ~/.cache/huggingface/lerobot/calibration/ (versioned copies live in the repo's hardware/calibration/).

If the arm later jumps to a weird pose That's a bad calibration or a mistyped name. Re-run calibration. It's two minutes — never live with a bad one.

Phase 3 · TeleopDrive the robot by hand

Goal of this phase: you can drive the follower smoothly with the leader, both cameras stream, and the scene is framed and fixed in place.

  1. Start teleoperation (no cameras yet). 
    lerobot-teleoperate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT \
    --robot.id=brick_follower \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=LEADER_PORT \
    --teleop.id=brick_leader

    Pick up the leader and move it slowly — the follower copies you. Ctrl-C stops it.

  2. Practice for 20 minutes.

    Pick things up. Put them down. Your smoothness here is the ceiling on your data quality, and your data quality is the ceiling on your policy. Jerky puppeteering = jerky robot, forever.

  3. Plug in both cameras and identify them.

    This one is a Terminal step — the tool saves a snapshot from each camera, which is the easiest way to tell them apart:

    lerobot-find-cameras opencv

    It lists each camera with a number (its "index"). Open the saved snapshots to see which number is which. Write down: wrist camera index = __, front camera index = __. The laptop's built-in camera also appears — ignore it. The commands below assume front = 0, wrist = 1 (this station's real values, recorded in SETUP-LOG.md) — change them if yours differ.

  4. Mount the cameras.
    • Wrist camera: on the arm near the gripper, looking at what the gripper is about to grab. Screw it down hard — glue it if it wobbles.
    • Front camera (the Acer webcam): on the desk-clamp camera arm, high and looking steeply down, so the table fills the frame and nothing beyond the table's back edge is visible.
  5. Open the live alignment view and frame the scene.

    The repo has a purpose-built tool for this (scripts/camera_align.py) — it shows both cameras live in the rerun viewer:

    python scripts/camera_align.py --front 0 --wrist 1 --width 640 --height 480

    While watching it, physically arrange the world until the front camera sees only what matters:

    • The brick, the target area, and the follower arm: fully visible at all times.
    • NOT visible: you, your keyboard, the leader arm, the neighbor's desk, clutter, harsh shadows.
    • Plain background; steady lamp light rather than daylight from a window.

    Leave the viewer running while you adjust. When it's right, don't touch anything and go to the next step.

  6. Fix everything in place. Permanently.

    Tighten the camera arm's knobs. Tape the cables. Check the follower's clamp. From this moment until your policy runs, nothing about the cameras or the arm's position may change. A camera nudged 2 cm invalidates every episode you've recorded.

  7. Stop the alignment viewer: Ctrl-C.

    Two programs can't share a camera — it must be closed before the lock script (next step) or teleop can use the feeds.

  8. Lock the front camera's settings.

    One command, run with no camera app open:

    scripts/lock_front_camera.sh

    What it does: the Acer A610 is fixed-focus, and its manual white balance is broken (forcing it off causes a green cast — so white balance stays auto, which is harmless under fixed lighting). The thing worth locking is exposure: the script measures the brightness that auto-exposure produces, switches to manual, and searches for the exposure value that matches it — then fixes the frame rate. Without this, the image pulses brighter and darker as the dark arm crosses the white table — noise in every frame. ⚠️ UVC settings can reset on replug or reboot, and the right exposure depends on lighting — run the script at the start of every recording session, after scene and lighting are final. (--auto reverts to full auto. Uses uvc-util, built once to ~/.local/bin.)

  9. Rehearse: teleop with cameras.

    The camera names front and wrist in this command matter — they're baked into every episode and the trained policy expects them forever:

    lerobot-teleoperate \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT \
    --robot.id=brick_follower \
    --robot.cameras="{ front: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 0, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}, wrist: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 1, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}}" \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=LEADER_PORT \
    --teleop.id=brick_leader \
    --display_data=true

    Practice the task looking only at the camera feeds in the rerun window — never at the robot. You are ready for Phase 4 when you can build the whole wall smoothly in under ~50 seconds — same build order (yellow, pink, orange), same grasps, no bumped blocks — several times in a row. 20+ minutes of practice is normal. Ctrl-C to stop.

  10. If teleop feels jittery, drop the live view.

    The viewer costs computer power. During plain practice you can run without --display_data=true for smoothness — but during recording you must watch the feeds, so rehearse with the viewer on at least some of the time.

Phase 4 · DataRecord your demonstrations

Goal of this phase: a dataset of ~50 good episodes of one task, checked and uploaded. The task: build a three-block wall — yellow and pink blocks end to end on the green mat, orange bridging on top of both. With a language-conditioned model like SmolVLA, the task sentence genuinely describes the goal, so it is written to match what happens — and it must stay word-for-word identical from recording through rollout.

  1. Set the scene.

    Green mat down (it is the visual anchor for "where the wall goes"). Each of the three blocks — yellow, pink, orange — gets one fixed home position, identical every episode. One warning about marks:

    Tape is visible to the robot too Anything in the camera image becomes part of what the policy learns. A taped target square is fine — if it is identical in every episode and still there when the policy runs. For the brick's starting spot, put your alignment mark outside the camera's view (e.g. on the table edge) so you can reset precisely without teaching the robot to depend on a mark.
  2. Decide your task sentence and never change it.

    Ours: build the block wall on the green mat in the order yellow, pink, orange - yellow and pink end to end, orange on top of both. SmolVLA reads this — keep it word-for-word identical at recording and at rollout.

  3. Start recording. 
    lerobot-record \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT \
    --robot.id=brick_follower \
    --robot.cameras="{ front: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 0, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}, wrist: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 1, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}}" \
    --teleop.type=so101_leader \
    --teleop.port=LEADER_PORT \
    --teleop.id=brick_leader \
    --display_data=true \
    --dataset.repo_id=sammy-labs/brickbot_so101_wall_v1 \
    --dataset.no_stamp=true \
    --dataset.num_episodes=50 \
    --dataset.single_task="build the block wall on the green mat in the order yellow, pink, orange - yellow and pink end to end, orange on top of both." \
    --dataset.episode_time_s=60 \
    --dataset.reset_time_s=25

    (no_stamp matters: without it, LeRobot appends a date-time tag to the dataset name, and every command that expects brickbot_so101_wall_v1 misses it.) What happens: recording of episode 1 starts. You have up to 60 seconds per episode (the wall takes longer than a single placement — end early with → when the orange block settles), then a 25-second break to reset all three blocks to their homes, then the next episode starts automatically. Three keys control everything:

    KeyWhat it does
    → (right arrow)"Done" — ends the episode now and accepts it. Press it the moment the brick is placed.
    ← (left arrow)"Redo" — throws away the current episode and restarts it. A redo is cheap, a bad episode is expensive.
    ESCStops the whole session cleanly, saves, and uploads.
  4. Record each episode by these rules.
    • Watch only the camera feeds on screen — never the physical robot. The policy will only ever see the feeds. If you can't do it from the feeds, it can't either.
    • Start moving immediately. A pause at the start teaches the robot to sit still.
    • Same style every time. Same approach path, same grasp. Open the gripper wide at the start; open it clearly wide when releasing.
    • Reset precisely during the 10-second break: brick back to its exact spot (use your out-of-view mark).
    • Bumped the target? Grabbed badly? Redo the episode. Only keep episodes you're happy with.
  5. Check the recording quality by replaying it (Terminal step).

    Replay makes the follower physically re-execute a recorded episode (place the brick at its start spot — the arm moves exactly as recorded):

    lerobot-replay \
    --robot.type=so101_follower \
    --robot.port=FOLLOWER_PORT \
    --robot.id=brick_follower \
    --dataset.repo_id=sammy-labs/brickbot_so101_wall_v1 \
    --dataset.episode=0

    Replay a handful of episodes (change the number). Smooth motion = good data. You can also watch all episodes as videos at the dataset visualizer — enter your dataset name (sammy-labs/brickbot_so101_wall_v1).

  6. Know your privacy setting.

    The dataset — including the videos of your desk — uploads to the Hugging Face Hub automatically. To keep it offline, add --dataset.push_to_hub=false to the record command (but cloud training then needs it on the Hub). Or after upload, open the dataset page on huggingface.co → Settings → change visibility to Private.

  7. To add more episodes later, resume — don't restart (Terminal step). 
    lerobot-record \
    ... same command as before ... \
    --resume=true \
    --dataset.root=~/.cache/huggingface/lerobot/sammy-labs/brickbot_so101_wall_v1 \
    --dataset.num_episodes=25   # number of ADDITIONAL episodes

Phase 5 · TrainingTrain the policy

Goal of this phase: a fine-tuned SmolVLA policy, stored on the Hugging Face Hub. Training needs a beefy graphics card (GPU) for an hour or so. Your plan: a rented cloud A100 via Hugging Face Jobs, paid from your loaded HF credit. Everything is launched from the Mac; no other computer is involved: the Mac records → the dataset uploads to the Hub → an A100 in HF's cloud trains it → the trained policy uploads back → the Mac runs it.

  1. Choose where to train.
    OptionWhenTime (6k steps)
    HF Jobs on an A10G ⭐ — --job.target=a10g-largeDefault choice — SmolVLA (450M) fine-tunes comfortably in 24 GB. Launched from the Mac, billed to the org.~1.5–2 h
    HF Jobs on an A100 — --job.target=a100-largeFaster, pricier; unnecessary at this model size~1 h
    Mac (Apple Silicon) — --policy.device=mpsPossible for SmolVLA but slow; cloud is the planovernight
  2. One-time check: Jobs access, org billing, and the exact hardware names.

    The credits live in the sammy-labs org, so jobs must run under that namespace — billing follows the namespace. Confirm access and list GPU flavors with current prices:

    hf jobs hardware

    Find the a10g entry and note its exact flavor name — this guide assumes a10g-large; if the listing shows a different name, use that. The training script (step 4) injects the org namespace so the job runs — and bills — under sammy-labs rather than your personal account. Two prerequisites: your org membership must have write permission, and if your access token is the fine-grained kind, it must include sammy-labs org access (a 403 on push = recreate the token with org scope). Useful companions: hf jobs logs <job-id>, hf jobs cancel <job-id> (billing stops with it), --job.timeout=4h as a runaway guard.

  3. Create a free Weights & Biases account (recommended).

    It gives you live charts of training progress. Sign up at wandb.ai, then in the Terminal:

    wandb login   # paste the API key from wandb.ai/authorize

    Don't want it? Use --wandb.enable=false in the next step.

  4. Run the training script — from the Mac's Terminal.

    One command; it fine-tunes SmolVLA on your dataset on a cloud GPU billed to the sammy-labs org:

    conda run -n lerobot python scripts/train_wall_smolvla.py          # full run, 6,000 steps
conda run -n lerobot python scripts/train_wall_smolvla.py 1000     # cheap smoke test

    Why a script: LeRobot's job submitter has no org-namespace option — it always bills the personal account (which has no credits → "402 Payment Required"). The script injects namespace=sammy-labs into the job API before launching the standard trainer, and carries the SmolVLA recipe: fine-tune from lerobot/smolvla_base (--policy.path, not a from-scratch policy type), batch 64, 6,000 steps on the 100-episode dataset, a checkpoint saved and pushed to the Hub every 1,000 steps (a failure at any point costs at most 1,000 steps — final pushes have failed before), policy landing at sammy-labs/brickbot_smolvla_v1. Expect ~1.5–2 h on the a10g. Afterwards, run scripts/tune_policy_for_serving.py once — it promotes the newest checkpoint to the repo root and squashes history so checkpoints never re-create the storage-quota problem.

  5. Watch the loss go down.

    On the wandb page, watch the loss chart. Good: falls steeply, then flattens. Fine-tuning loss won't reach zero — flat is the signal, not small. When it's been flat for a long while, further training buys nothing — better data beats more steps, always.

  6. Confirm the policy is on the Hub.

    Because of --policy.repo_id, the trained policy uploads automatically. Check that huggingface.co/sammy-labs/brickbot_smolvla_v1 exists. If it doesn't, upload the result by hand:

    hf upload sammy-labs/brickbot_smolvla_v1 outputs/train/brickbot_smolvla_v1/checkpoints/last/pretrained_model

Phase 6 · ShowtimeRun the policy

Goal of this phase: the robot does the task by itself — and you evaluate it the right way.

  1. Restore the scene exactly as trained.

    Same lighting, same cameras untouched, same mat, same brick. Any visible change is an unseen situation for the policy.

  2. Find the exact trained brick position (Terminal step).

    Run the replay command from Phase 4 on episode 0 and watch where the gripper goes to pick. Place the brick exactly there. This reconstructs a scene the policy definitely knows.

  3. Let the robot take over — via the cloud policy server.

    Why cloud: measured on 2026-08-13 (with the earlier 3B model), Mac-local inference could not hold the 30 Hz control target, so the policy runs on a rented GPU (HF Jobs, org-billed) and the Mac keeps only a lightweight robot client, connected through an SSH tunnel. SmolVLA is 7× smaller — once the pipeline is stable we can test whether Mac-local inference suffices and retire the server. Three commands, three terminals, in order:

    # 1. Start the cloud policy server (bills while running!)
conda run -n lerobot python scripts/serve_policy_cloud.py

# 2. Open the tunnel (own terminal, leave it running)
scripts/connect_policy_tunnel.sh

# 3. Run the robot client (arm moves!)
scripts/rollout_wall_async.sh

    Wait for the server job's logs to show it started (command printed by step 1) before step 3. The leader arm stays on the shelf; camera names and the task sentence are baked into the client script. When you finish a session, stop the server — hf jobs cancel <job-id> --namespace sammy-labs (the id is printed at step 1 and saved in scripts/.cloud_policy_job.json); it also auto-stops after 4 h as a safety net. One-time prerequisite: an SSH key registered at huggingface.co/settings/keys.

  4. Judge the result by this rule.
    • Works at the trained position: success! Run it 10 times; 8+/10 is a solid first policy.
    • Fails even at the trained position: something in the pipeline is broken — wrong calibration name, moved camera, wrong camera names, wrong policy path. Fix the pipeline. Do not touch training settings.
    • Works at the trained setup, fails when things move: common, though a pretrained VLA generalizes better than from-scratch models. Cure: record a v2 dataset with varied block start positions and retrain.
  5. Read the failure body-language.
    • Drifts toward familiar poses and hovers → it's in a situation it never saw.
    • Won't close the gripper → in training, the brick was never at that angle between the pincers.
    • Sits still → your dataset probably contains pauses. Record cleaner data.
Milestone 1 — complete when

The brick curriculum — from foam minis to real masonry

The numbers that shape the plan: standard clay brick 1.9–2.3 kg. Lightweight AAC block cut to brick size ~1 kg. Half-scale fired clay brick 250–400 g. 1:12 kiln-fired minis: grams. SO-101 payload: 200–500 g.

1now

Miniature masonry on the SO-101 — with your stacking-tower blocks

Your wooden tower blocks are the stage-1 bricks. Make the task harder, not the object: single placement → a straight course of blocks along a guide line with even 3–5 mm gaps → two stacked courses → a corner → the endgame party trick: rebuilding the tower itself, three blocks per layer, alternating direction each layer. A neat, evenly-spaced, squarely-stacked course IS bricklaying — in miniature. Then swap in 1:12–1:6 kiln-fired clay minis (real ceramic, different friction) and watch how much re-learning the change of material costs.

~$0–50 · uses Phases 0–6 as-is

2+2–3 mo

Half-scale real bricks — the SO-101's ceiling

Work up to 250–400 g half-scale fired clay bricks. Print a wider gripper with soft pads. Keep the work close to the robot's base — this weight is its ragged edge. Learn: stacking tolerances, chipped corners, and recovery demonstrations (deliberately record fixing a misplaced brick). When servos overheat or strain, stop. Don't fight the hardware.

~$50–150 · same pipeline, harder task

3overlaps

Adhesive skills — the mortar question, answered small

Skip traditional mortar (see next section — the professionals mostly did too). Build a servo-triggered glue-gun end effector. Practice with hot glue on stage-2 bricks: laying an even bead, clean starts and stops, pressing the brick to a set height. These are exactly the skills real robot masonry systems need.

~$100 tooling

4the goal

Real masonry on a bigger arm

Recommended target: AAC blocks + thin-bed adhesive on an AgileX PiPER arm (1.5 kg payload, ~$2,499). AAC is real, building-code-recognized masonry designed for light units, ±1 mm accuracy, and 1–3 mm glued joints — a system that happens to fit robots perfectly. Standard clay bricks need a Unitree Z1 Pro (4 kg, ~$15k) or used UR5e (~$30k+). You retrain the policy on the new arm — trained weights don't transfer between robots, but your datasets, tools, and skills all do.

~$3k credible real-masonry demo · $15–40k for standard clay brick

What carries over between stages — and what doesn't Carries over: the whole LeRobot pipeline, your recording rig and habits, training and evaluation know-how, task design. Doesn't: the trained model itself (each new arm = retrain), heavy-object physics intuition (light demos teach nothing about 2 kg swings), the gripper, and precision — hobby servos land within ±2–3 mm, real thin-bed masonry wants ±1 mm, and that is a hardware property no amount of training can fix.

Robot-friendly mortar: what the industry actually uses

Your instinct about "specialised mortar that robots can handle" is exactly right — and the industry's answer is mostly to abandon traditional mortar:

SystemHow it bonds bricksLesson for you
FBR Hadrian X (Australia)No mortar. The machine lays a bead of quick-curing construction adhesive; structural in ~45 min.Adhesive beats mortar for robots: no slump, thin bead, fast strength.
Monumental (Netherlands)Real mortar, extruded from a computer-controlled nozzle. Three robots work as a team; ~0.5 mm accuracy.Real mortar is solvable — but controlling its flow is their core company secret. Not a garage project.
SAM100 (USA)Mortar pumped to a nozzle that butters each brick; a human finishes the joints.Even the pragmatic approach keeps a human in the mortar loop.
AAC thin-bed mortar (ASTM C1660)Glue-like cement in 1–3 mm joints — possible because AAC blocks are accurate to ±1 mm.Your stage-4 system. Accurate blocks are what make joints robot-friendly.
PU masonry foam (Porotherm Dryfix, Tytan)A foam gun lays a bead on flat blocks; wall is loadable in hours.Most hobby-robot-friendly of all: a foam gun is a trivially easy robot tool. No pumps, no rheology.

What makes a bonding system robot-handleable: minutes of open working time, flows without separating, thin joints (no wiggle-and-squeeze mason technique needed), forgiving of small errors, and quick early strength so lower courses don't shift. PU foam and AAC thin-bed score best on all five. Trowel mortar scores worst.

Strategic note: every commercial system above uses CAD plans and position control — none uses learned policies. Learned masonry exists only in a handful of 2025 academic papers. Your imitation-learning route is genuinely fresh ground, and the industry's history says the hard parts are mortar flow, tolerances, and payload — which is exactly what the staged curriculum practices.
